## The Landscape

Kanha Tiger Reserve is the largest national park in Madhya Pradesh and the inspiration for Kipling's *The Jungle Book* — Seoni, the setting of the Mowgli stories, is just south of the park boundary. The landscape is classic Central Indian sal forest: tall, straight sal trees on the ridges, with open meadows called *maidans* sweeping through the valleys. These meadows are among the largest natural grasslands in Indian forest reserves, and they are the reason Kanha produces some of the most photogenic wildlife encounters in India — clean backgrounds, long sightlines, and golden light across open ground.

The Kanha and Kisli zones are the core photography areas. The Mukki zone in the south is less visited and worth considering for quieter safaris.

## Key Species

### Mammals

- **Bengal tiger** — Kanha's tiger population is robust and the habituation level of individuals in the core zones is high. The meadow encounters — a tiger walking across open grass at dawn with the forest edge behind — are Kanha's signature shots. Several tigresses with cubs are regularly located, and the playful cub sequences that are possible during a good encounter are exceptional.
- **Barasingha (swamp deer)** — Kanha is the primary conservation success story for this species; the population was fewer than 70 in the 1960s and is now over 800. The barasingha stag in hard antler from October to February is one of the most magnificent animals in Indian wildlife photography — large, richly coloured, with multi-tined antlers that can span 1.3 metres.
- **Indian wild dog (dhole)** — Kanha has healthy dhole packs that range across the meadows and are frequently encountered. Pack hunts across the open meadow are fast, chaotic, and extraordinary to witness and photograph.
- **Sloth bear** — present throughout; more likely in the rocky sections at the park edges.
- **Gaur** — large herds in the sal forest; bulls in the meadow at dawn are impressive subjects.
- **Chital, sambar, barking deer** — abundant prey species; the chital in Kanha's meadows are particularly photogenic — golden coats against green grass in morning light.
- **Indian leopard** — present but seen less often than tigers; the denser forest sections and the Supkhar zone are the better bets.

### Birds

- **Indian roller** — abundant on open perches; the blue flash in flight is a consistent subject.
- **Crested serpent eagle** — soars over every meadow clearing.
- **White-eyed buzzard** — a small, distinctive raptor of the drier forest edges.
- **Grey-headed fish eagle** — at the water bodies.
- **Lesser adjutant stork** — occasional at the larger water holes.
- **Indian pitta** (October–March) — ground-level in the understorey, stunning plumage.
- **Malabar pied hornbill** — in the taller forest sections.

## Photography Notes

Kanha's meadow system is the key differentiator from other Central India parks. For mammal photography, the early morning meadow sessions — where the grass catches the first light and animals are still active — are the highest-value photography windows. Position the vehicle at a meadow edge before dawn and wait.

For tiger photography specifically, the Kanha zone meadows (near Kanha village gate) and the Kisli zone roads have historically been the most productive. The Forest Department's elephant-assisted tiger relocation (where elephants find the tiger and vehicles are brought to the spot) gives very close encounters but at the cost of natural context — decide in advance whether this matters to you.

A 500–600 mm is standard for tigers in the forest. A 300–400 mm is often better for the open meadow encounters where context and environment improve the image.

The barasingha are most spectacular from November to February — hard antler, cooler temperatures, and low-angle morning light on the meadows combine to create conditions that are difficult to replicate in any Indian park.
