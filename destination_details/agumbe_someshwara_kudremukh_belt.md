## The Landscape

This belt of forests running through Shivamogga and Udupi districts represents the wettest part of the Western Ghats south of Goa — Agumbe receives among the highest rainfall in India outside the northeast. The forest here is true tropical wet evergreen: multilayered canopy, moss-draped branches, streams running through every valley, and a permanent green that persists even in the driest months.

Kudremukh National Park at the southern end is a different character — higher altitude grasslands and shola forest alternating across rolling peaks, with the Bhadra river originating in the interior. Someshwara Wildlife Sanctuary connects the two, protecting the lower-elevation riverine and coastal forest.

This is not a destination for large mammals. It is a destination for rainforest atmosphere, amphibians, reptiles, and a style of photography that is rare in India.

## Key Species

### Reptiles and Amphibians

- **King cobra** — Agumbe has one of India's highest king cobra densities. This is a serious reptile, and encounters without expert naturalists are inadvisable — but with a guide who knows the local individuals, it is possible to photograph one of the world's most dramatic animals in its natural setting. The herpetology research station at Agumbe (ARRS) is a useful contact point.
- **Malabar pit viper** — a beautifully patterned arboreal viper; found coiled on branches over streams. Multiple colour morphs exist — green, brown, and bluish individuals are all photographically distinct.
- **Indian rock python** — large individuals near the rivers.
- **Flying lizard (Draco)** — small but extraordinary; extends bright orange dewlaps and glides between trees.
- **Caecilians** — limbless burrowing amphibians, largely invisible but occasionally encountered on rainy nights.
- **Purple frog (Nasikabatrachus)** — found only in the Western Ghats; a bizarre, shovel-nosed fossorial frog that surfaces briefly to breed during the monsoon.
- **Fungoid frog, Malabar gliding frog** — both spectacular for macro photography.

### Birds

- **Malabar grey hornbill** — the endemic hornbill of the Western Ghats; smaller and more subtly coloured than its relatives, but a true endemic.
- **Sri Lanka frogmouth** — a cryptically patterned nocturnal bird, often found roosting in pairs on low branches during the day.
- **Malabar whistling thrush** — the loud, flute-like call along rocky streams; the electric-blue plumage flashes in the dappled light.
- **Broad-tailed grassbird** — found in the high grasslands of Kudremukh.
- **Nilgiri wood pigeon** — a large, striking endemic of the shola-grassland zone.

## Photography Notes

Monsoon photography (June–September) in Agumbe is a completely different discipline from dry-season wildlife photography. Rain, mist, and waterfalls are the visual language here. A weather-sealed camera body is essential. Macro subjects — frogs, insects, fungi — are at their most abundant and accessible. The mood is lush, saturated green with dramatic skies.

Post-monsoon (November–February) is more comfortable for general photography. The streams run clear, the snakes are more active, and access to Kudremukh's grassland ridges is manageable.

Landscape photography around Agumbe's famous sunsets (the "Agumbe sunset point" is a cliché but the view is genuinely excellent) and the Onake Abbi waterfall are worthwhile. Kudremukh peak, on a clear day, gives panoramic Western Ghats views.

For reptile photography, a red-light headlamp for night walks and a macro lens (90–105 mm) will serve better than a telephoto.

## Practical Notes

Accommodation is basic compared to Kabini or Bandipur. The Agumbe Rainforest Research Station offers simple rooms for serious naturalists. Homestays in Agumbe village are adequate. The roads from Shivamogga are driveable but slow after monsoon.
