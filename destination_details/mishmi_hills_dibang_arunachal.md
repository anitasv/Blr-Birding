## The Landscape

The Mishmi Hills and the Dibang Valley in eastern Arunachal Pradesh are among the most remote and least visited biodiversity hotspots in India. The Dibang river drains a vast catchment of temperate and alpine forest from the Tibetan border down to the Assam plains, and the valley is one of the few places in India where the forest is essentially intact from the foothills to the snowline.

The Mishmi Hills are named for the Mishmi people — the indigenous communities whose traditional territory covers most of the valley, and whose relationship with the forest has been central to its conservation. The forest here has not been logged at scale; the bird and mammal diversity reflects centuries of relatively undisturbed ecology.

This is an expedition-level destination. The roads are long, rough, and often impassable in monsoon. Permits are required (Inner Line Permit for Arunachal Pradesh). Infrastructure is sparse. But for photographers who want genuine frontier wildlife photography in India, the Dibang Valley offers a category of experience unavailable anywhere else on the subcontinent.

## Key Species

### Birds

- **Mishmi wren-babbler** — named for the region; found in the dense bamboo and rhododendron understorey at mid-elevations.
- **Sclater's monal** — a pheasant of the high-altitude mixed forest and meadow zone; the male's iridescent plumage in deep blues, greens, and copper ranks it among the most spectacular birds in Asia.
- **Temminck's tragopan, Blyth's tragopan** — both present at appropriate elevations.
- **Beautiful nuthatch** — on large moss-covered trunks in the temperate forest.
- **Ward's trogon** — in the temperate broadleaf zone.
- **Fire-tailed myzornis** — a tiny, extraordinary bird; the male is moss-green with a flame-orange tail. Found along streamsides in the temperate-alpine transition zone.
- **Rusty-bellied shortwing** — a small ground thrush of dense forest understorey.
- **Crested finchbill** — in subtropical forest.

### Mammals

- **Red panda** — the bamboo-oak forest at 2,000–3,000 m is prime red panda habitat; the Dibang Valley has one of the larger remaining wild populations. Elusive but genuinely possible on multi-day treks.
- **Clouded leopard** — present; one of the world's most beautiful cats but almost entirely nocturnal and extremely rarely photographed in the wild in India.
- **Asiatic black bear (Himalayan black bear)** — regularly encountered on the upper road sections.
- **Red goral** — a small, agile goat-antelope of the rocky slopes above 2,500 m.
- **Takin** — a massive, bizarre-looking bovid of the high-altitude bamboo and rhododendron forest; one of Asia's strangest large mammals.

## Photography Notes

The Dibang Valley's photography is fundamentally an expedition — planning around 7–10 days minimum gives enough time to work the altitudinal range from Roing at the valley floor to the higher-elevation camps at 2,500–3,000 m.

The road from Roing to Anini (the district headquarters) passes through multiple forest zones and is itself the primary photography route. Side tracks and forest paths above Anini access the higher-elevation species.

For red panda, early morning walks in the bamboo-oak forest around 2,200–2,500 m are the best strategy. A local Mishmi guide with knowledge of current red panda locations is essential; the species is crepuscular and largely arboreal.

Photography in this environment requires preparation for rapid weather changes: carry weather-sealed equipment, extra batteries (cold temperatures drain batteries quickly), and waterproof bags. The mountain light — low-angle, high-altitude clarity above the forest canopy — is extraordinary on clear days, with none of the haze that affects lowland photography.
