## The Landscape

Chilika Lake is the largest coastal lagoon in Asia — a shallow, brackish expanse of 1,100 sq km on the Odisha coast, separated from the Bay of Bengal by a narrow barrier spit. From November to February it hosts the largest concentration of migratory waterbirds in the Indian subcontinent, with over a million birds recorded in peak winter counts.

Mangalajodi is a small village on the northern shore of Chilika, at a point where the lake is exceptionally shallow — rarely more than a metre deep — and densely vegetated with aquatic grass and lotus. The village was transformed over the past two decades from a notorious bird-hunting area into one of India's most celebrated community-based bird conservation success stories. The former hunters became the guides and boat operators, and Mangalajodi is now the finest place in India to photograph open-water and marsh waterbirds from a boat at extremely close range.

## Key Species

- **Bar-headed goose** — arrives in November from the Tibetan plateau via the highest Himalayan passes; flocks of hundreds rest and feed in the shallows at Mangalajodi.
- **Greylag goose** — mixed with bar-headed in the flocks.
- **Northern pintail** — one of the most elegantly proportioned ducks; the elongated tail and chocolate head of the male are classic subjects. Abundant at Mangalajodi.
- **Gadwall, common teal, shoveler, garganey, tufted duck** — winter ducks in the thousands.
- **Purple swamphen** — large, vivid purple-and-red birds that walk on the floating vegetation with enormous yellow feet; very close approaches possible.
- **Pheasant-tailed jacana** — breeding birds in summer; winter birds retain much of the plumage.
- **Purple heron** — the most regal of the herons; hunts motionlessly in the reed edge.
- **Black-headed ibis** — flocks feed along the shore.
- **Great white pelican, Dalmatian pelican** — both recorded; the Dalmatian pelican is a globally vulnerable species.
- **Pied avocet, black-winged stilt** — on the exposed shallows.
- **Indian pond heron** — ubiquitous; the breeding-plumage transformation (from mottled brown to vivid orange-chestnut) between winter and summer is striking.
- **Grey-headed lapwing** — a winter visitor from China; present in the grassland areas around the lake edge.
- **Hen harrier, marsh harrier** — quarter the reed beds at dusk.

## Photography Notes

The boat photography at Mangalajodi is boat-level waterbird photography at its most refined. The boats are small, flat-bottomed, and paddled silently — the boatmen (former hunters who know every bird and its behaviour) manoeuvre within 5–8 metres of feeding and resting birds without causing flight.

This proximity, combined with the flat water surface and the soft morning light, produces waterbird images that are technically achievable nowhere else in India at this scale. The eye-level angle, with the water surface reflecting the sky beneath the birds, is the defining visual quality of Mangalajodi photography.

A 400–500 mm lens is ideal for individual portrait work. A 300 mm covers flocks in the wider context of the lake and the distant shore. Bring a wide-angle for the atmospheric dawn scenes — the boat silhouette against the sunrise, flocks lifting from the water, and the occasional morning mist that rolls across the northern lake surface.

Go out before sunrise and stay until around 9–9:30 am. The light window is short — two to three hours — but it is among the most productive photography windows in Indian birding. The midday hours are unproductive and very hot; a second short session around 4 pm before sunset can add golden-hour feeding shots.

The accommodation in Mangalajodi village is simple homestays; the Eco-tourism Mangalajodi Society manages bookings and guides. Bhubaneswar airport (100 km) is the entry point.
