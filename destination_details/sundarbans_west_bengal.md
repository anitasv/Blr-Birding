## The Landscape

The Sundarbans is the largest mangrove forest in the world — 10,000 sq km of tidal channels, mudflats, and dense mangrove across the delta of the Ganges, Brahmaputra, and Meghna rivers, split between India and Bangladesh. The Indian portion, in the South 24 Parganas district of West Bengal, covers about 4,300 sq km of which roughly half is the Sundarbans Tiger Reserve.

The landscape is entirely water-defined. There are no roads — movement is entirely by boat through a labyrinthine network of tidal creeks. The mangrove rises directly from the water on both banks, dense and impenetrable, its roots winding into the tidal mud. The sky is enormous, the water reflects everything, and the quality of light over the tidal channels at dawn and dusk is extraordinary.

The Sundarbans tiger is the most famous — and least seen — tiger in India. The population is real (estimates suggest 100–130 individuals), but the dense mangrove and the tidal flooding of large areas means most tigers remain invisible even to camera traps. A direct sighting is genuinely rare and cannot be planned for.

## Key Species

### Mammals

- **Bengal tiger** — the Sundarbans tiger is an ecologically distinct population adapted to swimming between islands, eating fish and crabs alongside deer, and tolerating saltwater. The extremely low sighting rate is frustrating but the ecological story is fascinating. When a tiger is seen — usually at the forest edge or briefly on a mudflat — the mangrove setting is unlike any other tiger photograph in existence.
- **Irrawaddy dolphin** — resident in the tidal channels; a small, blunt-nosed dolphin that surfaces and dives at moderate distances from boats. The calm water surface makes for clean shots when they surface predictably.
- **Gangetic dolphin** — also present in the larger channels; the long beak is characteristic.
- **Smooth-coated otter** — families fish in the creeks.
- **Spotted deer (chital)** — herds on the grassy banks and the drier island interiors; an important tiger prey species here.
- **Wild boar** — common on the island interiors.

### Birds

- **Mangrove pitta** — a stunning small pitta of dense mangrove; the blue, green, and red plumage is vivid against the dark roots. Found by its sharp call in the denser forest sections.
- **Collared kingfisher** — the mangrove specialist; perches on exposed roots and branches at the water's edge.
- **Brown-winged kingfisher** — a large, rufous-brown kingfisher of the mangrove zone; rarely seen outside this habitat.
- **Ruddy kingfisher** — richly orange-rufous, a forest kingfisher of the wetter areas.
- **Masked finfoot** — an extraordinarily secretive and rare waterbird; present in the quieter creek systems.
- **Lesser adjutant stork** — regularly seen on the mudflats at low tide.
- **Osprey** — hovers and dives over the tidal channels in winter.
- **White-bellied sea eagle** — soars over every wide channel.
- **Plum-headed parakeet, alexandrine parakeet** — in the taller forest sections.

## Photography Notes

Boat position is everything in the Sundarbans. The tidal state determines which mudflats and banks are exposed and which are submerged — plan the day around the tidal schedule, positioning at exposed mudflats during low tide (where deer and birds concentrate on the mud) and exploring the channels during mid-tide.

Dawn on the Sundarbans tidal channels — the flat water, the mist from the land-sea temperature differential, the call of the brown-winged kingfisher, and the slow gold of the sun rising over the mangrove line — is one of the finest atmosphere photography experiences in India, independent of any wildlife encounter.

For kingfisher photography, identify exposed perch sticks and roots at likely locations and maintain a slow, motionless boat position 20–30 metres away. Boat engine noise and movement are the primary disturbance factors.

The Sundarbans is best accessed from Kolkata via Gosaba or Sajnekhali; most photographers join a 3–4 day boat-based expedition organised by registered operators. The Sajnekhali visitor centre and the Sudhanyakhali watchtower are the main fixed-point wildlife observation locations.
