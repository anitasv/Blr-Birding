## The Landscape

Ladakh is India's high-altitude desert, occupying the trans-Himalayan rain shadow beyond the main Himalayan ranges. The landscape is starkly beautiful: bare, mineral-coloured mountains in shades of ochre, rust, and grey, carved by glacial rivers into wide valleys. The Indus and Zanskar rivers provide the only green threads in this otherwise treeless, rainfall-starved terrain. Buddhist monasteries perch on impossible crags above the valleys, and the clear, high-altitude light gives a photographic quality to even ordinary subjects.

Hemis National Park, the largest national park in India by area (4,400 sq km), covers the southern Ladakh ranges above the Indus valley. It is the primary habitat in India for the **snow leopard**.

## Key Species

### Mammals

- **Snow leopard** — Hemis has one of the highest snow leopard densities in the world, though this is relative — the total population within the park is perhaps 40–70 individuals in a vast, rugged area. Winter (January–March) is the only practical time for snow leopard photography: prey animals (bharal) descend to lower altitudes, the snow leopards follow, and the white-and-grey spotted coat stands out against the brown winter hillsides rather than disappearing into summer vegetation. Snow leopard photography expeditions based at Ulley, Rumbak, and Hemis villages are well-established; several wildlife photography operators run dedicated winter expeditions. Sightings are not guaranteed on any given day, but multi-week expeditions in a good year can produce several extended encounters.
- **Himalayan snow leopard** cubs, if a female has a litter, are among the most extraordinary photography subjects in Asia.
- **Bharal (Himalayan blue sheep)** — the primary prey of the snow leopard; large herds on the rocky slopes are reliable year-round.
- **Himalayan ibex** — massive curved horns and a sure-footed presence on the steepest rock faces.
- **Tibetan wolf** — present and occasionally seen in the same valleys as the snow leopards.
- **Pallas's cat** — extremely rare but recorded from Hemis; a flat-faced, thick-furred small cat of the boulder fields.
- **Eurasian lynx** — another secretive felid recorded from the park.
- **Himalayan marmot** — large, colonial, and conspicuous in summer; hibernates from October to May.
- **Kiang (Tibetan wild ass)** — in the high Changthang plateau east of Leh; not technically Hemis but accessible on the same trip.

### Birds

- **Golden eagle** — the apex raptor of the Ladakh skies; large, soaring individuals are frequent.
- **Lammergeier (bearded vulture)** — spectacular in flight with its long diamond-shaped tail and rust underparts.
- **Himalayan griffon vulture** — enormous, soaring in groups.
- **Eurasian eagle-owl** — the world's largest owl; roosts in cliff crevices.
- **Tibetan snowcock** — a large gallinaceous bird of the high ridges; its descending call carries across the valleys.
- **Robin accentor, rufous-streaked accentor** — small ground birds of the boulder field.
- **Horned lark** — in the high-altitude grassland.
- **Black-necked crane** (Changthang, winter) — a large, elegant crane of the high-altitude wetlands.

## Photography Notes

Summer photography (June–September) gives comfortable conditions, full-day daylight, and access to the high passes. The landscape, the culture, and the wildlife other than snow leopards are the primary subjects. The Changthang plateau east of Leh — accessed via the Manali-Leh highway or flights to Leh — holds kiang, Tibetan gazelle, and black-necked cranes at the high-altitude lakes (Pangong Tso, Tso Moriri).

Winter snow leopard expeditions (January–March) require preparation for temperatures of -15 to -25°C at base camps, multi-day walks into the valleys, and extended patience on cold hillsides. Long telephoto (600 mm +) on a stable tripod is standard. The snow leopard's habit of resting on prominent rocks and cliff edges — surveillance behaviour for hunting bharal — means that once located, they can be observed and photographed for hours.

The Buddhist monastery photography — Hemis, Thiksey, Diskit, Lamayuru — is exceptional year-round, with the best light in the clear post-monsoon period (October–November) and the dramatic winter snow adding atmosphere to the monastery landscapes.

Acclimatisation to altitude (3,500 m at Leh) requires at least two rest days before exerting yourself; plan this into the itinerary.
