## The Landscape

Kaziranga National Park occupies the floodplain of the Brahmaputra river in central Assam, a mosaic of tall elephant grass, seasonal swamps, and riverine forest. The Brahmaputra floods the park almost completely every monsoon, and the annual cycle of inundation and drying shapes everything — the grasslands, the wildlife density, and the extraordinary concentration of large animals that makes Kaziranga one of the most productive wildlife photography destinations in Asia.

The landscape is flat, expansive, and dominated by *Saccharum* elephant grass that grows to 4 metres — taller than an elephant. The open patches between the grass are where the action is: shallow wetlands full of swamp deer and rhinos, open floodplain grassland grazed by buffalo herds, and the riverine forest edges where tigers hunt.

Kaziranga has more Bengal tigers per unit area than any other park, but it is not primarily a tiger destination — the grassland makes sightings difficult. The undisputed stars are the rhinos.

## Key Species

### Mammals

- **Indian one-horned rhinoceros** — Kaziranga holds over 2,600 rhinos, roughly 70% of the world population. They are abundant, visible, and relatively tolerant of vehicles. The sight of multiple rhinos scattered across open grassland at dawn — sometimes 10 or 15 visible from a single position — is a spectacle without parallel anywhere in Asia. The single horn, the deeply folded skin that looks like armour plate, and the massive, prehistoric bulk make this one of the most compelling photographic subjects in India.
- **Asian elephant** — very large herds; Kaziranga's elephants are wilder and less habituated than those in South Indian parks, which gives encounters a more unpredictable, dynamic quality.
- **Swamp deer (barasingha)** — large herds in the wetland grasslands; the stags in winter antler are dramatic subjects.
- **Wild water buffalo** — the wild ancestor of the domestic buffalo; Kaziranga holds one of the last viable wild populations. These are massive, dangerous animals — taller and heavier than domestic buffalo, with enormous swept-back horns. Herds of 20–50 move through the open grassland zones.
- **Hoolock gibbon** — in the forest sections; the only ape in India, with a haunting whooping call at dawn.
- **Bengal tiger** — present throughout but seen most often near the Central Zone roads and the Kohora area.

### Birds

- **Swamp francolin** — a grassland bird endemic to the alluvial plains; Kaziranga is one of the most reliable locations.
- **Bengal florican** — critically endangered grassland bustard; a small population persists in the open grass zones.
- **Pallas's fish eagle** — a large, powerful eagle of the wetlands; the rich chestnut-and-white plumage against the sky is striking.
- **Grey-headed fish eagle** — common at the water bodies.
- **Baer's pochard** — a critically endangered diving duck; occasionally recorded at Kaziranga's wetlands in winter.
- **Greater adjutant stork** — Assam is the last stronghold; large, prehistoric-looking birds with bare, pendulous neck pouches.
- **Spot-billed pelican, painted stork** — at the larger water bodies.
- **Hen harrier, pallid harrier** — quarter the grassland in winter.

## Photography Notes

Kaziranga has four zones: Central (Kohora), Western (Bagori), Eastern (Agratoli), and Burapahar. The Central Zone has the highest rhino density and the most open terrain — this is the first-choice zone for photography. The Western Zone is good for elephants and buffalo. The Eastern Zone is quieter and excellent for birding.

Jeep safaris at dawn are the standard format. Elephant-back safaris (now limited and managed by the Forest Department) give access into the tall grass where rhino are otherwise invisible from jeeps, but the unstable platform limits photography quality.

For rhino photography, the shallow wetland areas at dawn — where rhinos stand in golden-lit water with the early mist still on the grass — produce the iconic Kaziranga images. A 400–500 mm covers the medium-distance rhino work; a 300 mm or wider for herd-in-landscape compositions.

The monsoon flood retreat in October–November concentrates animals as they return to the exposed grassland — this transitional period, just as the season opens, often has the highest density of visible wildlife.
