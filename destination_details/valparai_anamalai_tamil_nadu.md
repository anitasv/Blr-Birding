## The Landscape

Valparai is a small hill town at 1,000 m in the Anamalai Hills, accessed by a famous 40-hairpin road from Pollachi. The town is surrounded by Tata Tea estates that have existed since the colonial era, and the forest fragments — shola patches and riparian corridors between the tea — function as wildlife habitat for a surprisingly dense population of large animals. The Anamalai Tiger Reserve (Indira Gandhi Wildlife Sanctuary) borders the estates to the north and east.

What makes Valparai special is the unusual coexistence of wildlife and human settlement. Lion-tailed macaques walk across estate roads. Great hornbills fly over the tea factory. A Nilgiri tahr might be grazing on the lawn of the estate bungalow. The habituation level of wildlife here, combined with the misty, green aesthetic of the tea landscape, creates a distinctive photography context unlike any other in India.

## Key Species

### Mammals

- **Lion-tailed macaque** — Valparai is one of the best places in India to see and photograph this Endangered endemic primate. The distinctive silver mane framing the black face is unmistakeable. The NCF (Nature Conservation Foundation) has been monitoring specific troops for years, and their movement maps help locate them reliably. Groups of 10–20 individuals move through the tea estate corridors and road edges daily.
- **Nilgiri tahr** — present on the rocky ridges at the higher elevation sections of the Anamalai reserve; more effort required than at Eravikulam but possible.
- **Asian elephant** — large herds move through the Anamalai reserve and occasionally through the estate corridors. Road crossing elephants are a regular feature of the Valparai experience.
- **Gaur** — regularly seen at the forest-estate edge in the early morning.
- **Indian leopard** — present; occasionally seen crossing estate roads at dawn and dusk.

### Birds

- **Great hornbill** — in numbers. The tall forest trees within the estate corridors support breeding pairs, and the birds fly low over the tea regularly. One of the most accessible locations for great hornbill photography in South India.
- **Malabar pied hornbill** — also present; smaller than the great hornbill but equally striking.
- **Nilgiri wood pigeon** — common at the shola forest edges.
- **Black-and-orange flycatcher** — shola interiors; a vivid, compact endemic.
- **Nilgiri flycatcher** — males at the grassland-shola interface.
- **Indian grey hornbill** — at lower elevations near Pollachi.

## Photography Notes

The road-based photography here is excellent. The winding estate roads through the tea are passable by car, and the lion-tailed macaques cross or sit near the road frequently. A 300–500 mm lens is useful for the macaques in the canopy, but troops on the ground in the open tea fields can be photographed with shorter focal lengths.

The misty morning light over the tea estates — before 8 am in the cooler months — gives a mood that is very different from the harsh tropical light. The green rows of tea, white mist, and dark forest backdrop make strong landscape-with-wildlife compositions possible even with a standard zoom.

For hornbills, the road from the top of the 40-hairpin pass into Valparai and the estate roads near Sholayar dam are the most productive zones. Fruiting trees concentrate the birds; ask local naturalists about current hotspots.

The NCF Valparai project (nature.org.in) has produced excellent data on lion-tailed macaque movements; contacting them in advance for current troop locations is worthwhile for serious photographers.
