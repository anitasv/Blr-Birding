## The Landscape

Gir National Park in the Saurashtra peninsula of Gujarat is the last place on Earth where the **Asiatic lion** survives in the wild. The forest is dry deciduous teak, with a network of seasonal rivers — the Hiran, Shingoda, and Machhundri — cutting through the hills. The landscape is more varied than its flat surroundings suggest: rocky gorges, riverine forest with tall ficus and acacia, and open scrub grassland where the lions hunt.

The lions of Gir are genetically distinct from their African relatives and have been isolated here for over a century. They are smaller, with a less prominent mane in the males, and they carry a fold of skin along the belly (an abdominal fold) not seen in African lions. Photographing an Asiatic lion — an animal that was extinct everywhere else on Earth by the 1960s — has a significance beyond the image itself.

## Key Species

### Mammals

- **Asiatic lion** — population has recovered from fewer than 20 individuals in 1900 to over 600 today. Prides of 2–8 individuals are the norm (African prides are larger). The best zones for lion photography are the Sasan core zone and the Devalia Safari Park, the latter offering very high guarantee of sightings in a smaller, enclosed area. For wild-context photography, the core Gir zone jeep safaris are the right choice. Dawn is the best time — lions are active after a night of hunting and frequently visible on the roads and rocky outcrops.
- **Chital (spotted deer)** — abundant; the primary prey of the Gir lions.
- **Sambar** — less common than chital but present near the rivers.
- **Nilgai** — common on the open scrub.
- **Indian leopard** — Gir has a healthy leopard population, present alongside the lions; the two species largely partition the habitat, with leopards more common in rocky zones.
- **Striped hyena** — regularly seen at dusk and dawn; Gir has one of the more accessible hyena populations in India.
- **Jungle cat** — a small, grey-brown felid frequently seen on the open tracks at dawn.
- **Indian pangolin** — extremely rare and nocturnal; Gir is known to have a population.

### Birds

- **Indian scops owl, mottled wood owl** — roosting in large trees.
- **Crested serpent eagle** — a common soaring raptor.
- **Bonelli's eagle** — breeds in the rocky gorges.
- **Painted sandgrouse** — flocks in the open scrub.
- **Indian robin, Indian pitta** (winter) — both common in the understorey.

## Photography Notes

Getting a good Asiatic lion photograph requires a 500–600 mm lens for the full-frame portrait from typical safari vehicle distances. In Devalia Safari Park the distances are shorter (300–400 mm sufficient) but the setting feels less wild.

For the most interesting behavioural shots — lions at the river crossings, cubs playing in the morning light, a lioness watching from a rocky vantage — plan for multiple safaris across two days in the main Gir zone. The Hiran riverine area is particularly scenic; the river crossings give the kind of full-body, clean-background lion shots that are difficult anywhere else.

The Maldhari pastoral communities still live inside Gir, and their circular nesses (settlements) surrounded by thorny fences are a documentary photography subject worth exploring with permission. Their relationship with the lions is extraordinary — lions occasionally take cattle from their pens, and yet the Maldharis are among the strongest advocates for lion conservation.

April–May is hot but lions concentrate near water and sightings are at their most predictable. December–March gives better temperatures and cleaner light.
